Compared with the coinholder stack

The two systems answer different questions. They are also both voting systems for the same ecosystem, so they can be compared property by property. Every claim about the coinholder stack links to the public sentence it rests on, in the draft ZIPs and code that have been public since March 2026. Where we derive something from several sentences, we say so.

The table

Is the weight hidden? The weight is split into 16 encrypted shares, each sent to half of the servers, every share carrying the same list of commitments for the 16.

The three points that matter

1. Who holds the key to the outcome

In the coinholder stack, the key that opens every ballot is held, in shares, by the organizations with the most at stake in the outcome, and the same organizations run the servers that receive the shares. The privacy of a voter's balance rests on those servers keeping their logs apart, while the delivery rule sends every share to half of them.

In Zcash Agora the key is drand's. The threshold is held by a consortium that has no stake in Zcash and does not know this poll exists, the round it produces is public for everyone at once, and the most that threshold could ever do is open the ballots a little early: there is no balance in them, no identity, and nothing it could alter. The trust assumption is moved to a party with nothing to gain and reduced to a question of timing.

2. What is actually encrypted

In the coinholder stack the choice is public and the weight is encrypted: the exact balance of a shielded Zcash user, the one quantity the Zcash protocol is built to hide, is what the voting layer puts under a committee key. In Zcash Agora the ballot is a choice and nothing else, hidden until the close and pseudonymous after it.

3. What the count depends on

The coinholder count depends on a list of spent notes computed by one party and accepted on one signature. The Agora count depends only on what is in the public log: every eligibility proof, every ballot, every refusal. Less machinery was needed, not more: no committee, no ceremony, no private information retrieval, no chain of our own.

Scope of this comparison

  • It compares the two designs as published. It makes no statement about any operator's conduct; a threshold assumption leaves no trace either way.
  • Agora's own exposures are stated on every page: a relay sees an address, registering takes a face match, and every ballot is readable at the close under its pseudonym.
  • Passports are one measure of personhood, and this is one count next to another. One eye open is better than none.
