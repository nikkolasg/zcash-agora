The protocol

Zcash Agora is a deployment of isoloir, an open protocol for anonymous polls with passport eligibility. This page gives the parties, the steps of a vote, and what each party can learn. Nothing is used before it is introduced. The normative text is the specification. The threat model is the design note. The files of this deployment are in the repository.

1. The parties

Eight parties take part. Four are ours or chosen by us. Four are outside Zcash. No party alone can read a ballot before the close or tie a ballot to a person.

1.1 The voter

The voter has a passport with a chip, a phone with NFC and a browser. The phone runs the ZKPassport app. The app reads the chip and proves facts about it. The browser runs the voting page. The page holds the voter secret, builds ballots and verifies the log. Only the voter holds the secret (parties).

1.2 The issuing state and ZKPassport

The state signed the chip of the passport. The signature chain runs from the state's authority to the document. The state keeps a record of each passport it issued. ZKPassport maintains the app and SDK and open circuits that verify the chain on the phone. Its servers relay the proof from the phone to the browser, encrypted. ZKPassport is trusted for correct circuits, which are public. It is not trusted for secrecy.

1.3 The OPRF network

A proof of a valid passport does not stop a second registration with the same passport. The proof must also disclose a number unique to the document. ZKPassport offers two. The plain identifier is a hash of the chip data. Whoever holds the chip data can recompute it. The issuing state does. The salted identifier adds a secret that no one holds in full. A network of independent servers evaluates an oblivious pseudo-random function on a blinded input from the phone. Each server holds a share of the key. The servers learn neither the input nor the output. A threshold of servers can evaluate the function on any input. Fewer learn nothing. Zcash Agora uses the salted identifier under ZKPassport's key 1, pinned in the registry. ZKPassport requires a face match for this mode (registries).

1.4 The relays

Voters send nothing to a server of ours. Each message is a signed event on public Nostr relays run by others. This ledger names three. A relay stores and forwards. It sees the sender's network address and the bytes. It keeps a copy for anyone who asks. The copy proves a dropped message (channels).

1.5 The ledger and the mirror

The ledger is our one server, the ingest. It reads the relays and checks each message. It appends each message to one signed log, accepted or refused with the reason. It holds the log's signing key and no other key. The log is published as files in a public git repository, the mirror. Browsers read the mirror by commit hash. The ingest is the only writer (the log, the mirror).

1.6 drand

drand is a public randomness beacon. The League of Entropy runs it: Cloudflare, EPFL, Kudelski Security, Protocol Labs, the Ethereum Foundation, UCL, the University of Chile and others. Every three seconds the nodes sign the round number with a threshold key. The signature of a future round does not exist before its time. A published signature stays public. Time-lock encryption uses a round's signature as the decryption key (tlock, paper). The League does not know that this poll uses its beacon.

1.7 Bitcoin

The ledger stamps each state of its log with OpenTimestamps. A stamp commits a hash into a Bitcoin block. The ledger reads block headers and writes through the public calendars. No coins move.

1.8 The authority and the auditor

The authority opens the ballots after the close and posts the count. Under a time-lock it holds no key. It fetches the drand round, opens each ballot and posts the result. The ingest re-checks the result before it logs it. Ours runs hourly. Anyone can run one. Two correct authorities post the same entry. The auditor recomputes everything from the log, the relays and the mirror. Your browser is one (the audit).

2. The vote, step by step

2.1 Setup

Three documents define a deployment. They are the first entries of the log. The ledger entry names the origin, the signing key and the relays. The registry is the eligibility rule: here, any passport ZKPassport verifies, unexpired, no age or nationality rule, salted identifier under a pinned OPRF key. The poll names its registry, the question, the options, the opening and closing times, the drand round of the close and the hash of the ballot circuit. A registry's identifier settings never change: a change would give each passport a second identifier (ledger entry, registries, polls, this deployment's files).

2.2 The voter secret

The browser makes one secret per registry. It derives the secret from a passkey through the WebAuthn PRF extension when the platform offers it, otherwise from twelve BIP39 words. The secret stays on the device. Everything the voter publishes derives from it by one-way functions (voter secret).

2.3 Registration

Registration adds one leaf to the registry tree: a commitment to the voter secret.

  1. The browser draws a one-time key. It sends the phone a request bound to that key.
  2. The phone reads the chip, checks the state's signature chain, does the face match, gets the salt from the OPRF network on a blinded query, and makes a proof. The proof discloses three things: the document is valid and unexpired today; the one-time key; the salted identifier. Nothing else leaves the phone (personhood).
  3. The browser derives the leaf from the secret, signs the registration with the one-time key and posts it to a relay.
  4. The ingest verifies the proof with ZKPassport's open verifier. It checks the proof's OPRF key against the registry. It refuses an identifier the log already holds. It appends the leaf. It logs each refusal with the reason.

The identifier is public in the log. That lets anyone check that no passport registered twice. Section 3.4 says what it reveals (registration).

2.4 The ballot

A ballot proves two facts: a registered voter made it, and that voter made no other. One zero-knowledge proof does both, written in Noir and proven with UltraHonk in the browser. Its public values are: a recent root of the registry tree; the poll; a pseudonym; a commitment to the choice; the hash of the sealed choice (2.5). The pseudonym derives from the secret and the poll. It is constant for one voter in one poll and unlinkable across polls. The proof states that a leaf under the root derives from the secret behind the pseudonym, and that the committed choice is one option or the blank. The ingest accepts one ballot per pseudonym per poll (statement, checks). UltraHonk needs no ceremony. Anyone can rebuild the circuit with the generator and compare the key hash the poll pins.

2.5 The seal

The choice is sealed under a fresh symmetric key. That key is encrypted with tlock to the drand round of the close. Before that round, no one can open a ballot, us included. The proof binds the hash of the sealed blob. The circuit does not verify the encryption: time-lock arithmetic costs too much inside a proof. The opening checks it instead. A blob that does not open to its commitment makes the ballot void: listed, not counted. Only the voter's own software can produce such a blob (encryption).

2.6 Transport and receipts

The browser posts registrations and ballots as signed events under one-time keys. It ranks the relays by a value derived from the secret. The registration goes to the first relay, the ballots to the second. One relay does not see both. The ingest answers each message with a signed acknowledgement that names the checkpoint that will hold it. It posts the acknowledgement on the same relay. The browser keeps the receipt and checks the checkpoint later. A missing message is a complaint that anyone can judge from the relay's copy and the ingest's signature (acknowledgements, receipts).

2.7 The log, the mirror, the stamps

The log is an RFC 6962 Merkle tree. In each minute in which the log grew, the ingest signs a checkpoint in the C2SP signed-note format: the log's size and root, each registry's size and root, the mirror commit. Entries in a checkpoint are ordered by hash, not by arrival. Each checkpoint is stamped with OpenTimestamps until it carries a Bitcoin attestation. The mirror is files read by commit. Setups and results carry inclusion proofs. The mirror cannot serve a key or a result that the log does not hold (the log, the mirror).

2.8 The close and the opening

At the first checkpoint after the closing time, the ingest logs a close entry. No ballot is accepted after it. When the drand round arrives, the authority fetches the beacon and verifies it under the chain's key. It opens each counted ballot and checks it against its commitment. It posts the tally: the totals, the count, the void list, the beacon. The ingest repeats each opening before it logs the entry. The beacon is public. Anyone can repeat the computation. Two correct tallies are identical (tally, close).

2.9 The audit

The audit replays the ledger's judgment: each checkpoint signature and the consistency between checkpoints; the stamps against Bitcoin headers; each registration proof and identifier; each ballot proof and pseudonym; each refusal's reason; the beacon and each opening; the mirror's files at each commit. With the relays, it checks that each message they hold was judged. A check that cannot run is a failure. The audit runs in the browser and from one script (the audit).

3. Privacy analysis

3.1 The three links

  • Passport to leaf. The registration proof discloses only the salted identifier. This link is hidden as long as the identifier cannot be recomputed.
  • Leaf to ballot. Hidden by the ballot's membership proof, under the assumptions of UltraHonk on BN254.
  • Ballot to choice. Hidden until the drand round by the time-lock. Public afterwards, under the pseudonym.

At the close, every ballot opens under its pseudonym. The pseudonym names no one. A voter who gives up the secret can prove which ballot is theirs. The protocol reveals choices without persons.

Everyone honest. "Identifier" means the salted passport identifier. "Every ballot" means each ballot's choice and pseudonym, at the round and after.

3.3 What a corrupted party can do

Each item lists everything the party can do beyond section 3.2. Nothing else.

  • The ledger can refuse service. It can keep two signed histories and show different readers different ones. A fork is detectable: two conflicting checkpoint notes prove it, and the audit collects notes from the relays and the mirror (fork detection).
  • A relay can pair an address with each post it carries: an identifier for a registration; a pseudonym for a ballot, and its choice at the round. Two relays, or one relay and a network observer, can pair an identifier with a choice through an address. An address names a person only when the network does.
  • The OPRF servers, at the threshold, hold the key and can evaluate the function on any input. With a passport's chip data from elsewhere, they can recompute its identifier (3.4).
  • drand, at the threshold, can sign a round early. It then reads every ballot before the close, under pseudonyms.
  • ZKPassport can, through a flaw in its circuits, admit an invalid passport. The circuits are public and pinned by hash.
  • The voter's device, seized or compromised, gives the secret, and with it every pseudonym and ballot of the voter. The twelve words give the same. A voter who gives the words to a buyer gives a receipt.

3.4 What the issuing state can learn

The state knows the chip data of each passport it issued. It reads the public log.

  • Plain identifier: the state recomputes the identifier of each passport and reads off which of its citizens registered. Zcash Agora does not use this mode.
  • Salted identifier, honest OPRF network: nothing. The salt is held in shares by servers that answer only blinded queries.
  • Salted identifier, corrupted OPRF threshold: the servers supply the function, the state the inputs. Together they recompute each identifier and read off which citizens registered.

In every case the state learns at most participation. The OPRF touches registration only. The ballot's membership proof and the time-lock are unaffected. A state that also controls a relay, or observes its citizens' networks, can pair an address with a choice. Tor or a VPN closes that path.

3.5 What nobody can do

  • Register twice with one passport: one identifier per document, and the log refuses a second.
  • Vote twice in one poll: one pseudonym per secret and poll, and the log refuses a second.
  • Vote without a leaf: the ballot proof needs one.
  • Alter a ballot in transit: the proof binds the sealed blob's hash.
  • Read a ballot before the round: the key does not exist.
  • Miscount: the tally is a function of the log and the beacon, and anyone recomputes it.
  • Drop a message unnoticed: the relay's copy and the receipt prove the omission.
  • Rewrite history unnoticed: checkpoints are signed, consistent and stamped in Bitcoin.

3.6 Limits

  • Ballots open in clear at the round, under pseudonyms. Hence one ballot per voter and no correction in this mode (the matrix).
  • A fork of the log is detectable, not prevented. Pinning one history on a chain is designed, not deployed (design).
  • Network metadata is the one exposure the protocol does not close by itself.
  • Passports only, with a chip and an NFC phone, and a face match for the salted identifier.
  • Not post-quantum. The time-lock is pairing-based. The proofs are on BN254. Ballots are public at the round in any case; a quantum computer could in principle attack the proofs that hide the two other links.
  • Scale: about a hundred thousand voters and ballots with the current software (limits).
