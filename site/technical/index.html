The protocol

Zcash Agora is a deployment of isoloir, an open protocol for anonymous polls with passport eligibility. This page tells the protocol as a paper would: who the parties are, what happens at each step of a vote, and then what each party can learn, alone or in collusion. Nothing is used before it is introduced. The normative text is isoloir's specification; the threat model is its design note; the files of this deployment are in the Zcash Agora repository.

1. The parties

Eight parties take part in a poll. Four of them are ours or chosen by us; four are outside anyone's control in Zcash. None of them, alone, can read a ballot before the close or tie a ballot to a person.

1.1 The voter and their devices

A voter has a passport with a chip, a phone that can read it over NFC, and a browser. The phone runs the ZKPassport app, which reads the chip and proves things about it without disclosing it. The browser runs the voting page, a static page that holds the voter's secret, builds ballots and verifies the log. The two talk through a QR code or a link. The voter is the only party that ever holds their own secret (parties).

1.2 The issuing state and ZKPassport

The state that issued the passport signed its chip: a certificate chain from the state's authority down to the document. It is the root of eligibility, and it is also the party with the most knowledge about the voter, since it keeps a record of every passport it signed. ZKPassport, the company, maintains the app and SDK that verify that chain on the phone, a public set of circuits and verifiers, and a registry of the states' certificates whose root is read from Ethereum by any verifier. The proof is produced on the phone; the ZKPassport servers carry it to the browser through an encrypted bridge and never see its content. ZKPassport is a party we rely on for the correctness of its circuits, which are open, not for secrecy.

1.3 The OPRF network

A proof that says "this is a valid passport" is not enough to stop one passport from registering twice; the proof must also disclose a number unique to the document. ZKPassport offers two kinds. The plain identifier is a hash of the chip's data: anyone who holds that data, which includes the issuing state, can recompute it. The salted identifier mixes in a secret that nobody holds in full: an oblivious pseudo-random function evaluated by a small network of independent servers, each holding a share of the key, on a blinded input from the phone. The servers learn neither the input nor the output; the phone learns the output and nothing about the key. Fewer than a threshold of them know nothing; a threshold of them, together, can evaluate the function on any input. Zcash Agora uses the salted identifier under ZKPassport's published key 1, pinned in the registry, and ZKPassport requires a face match in the app for this mode (registries).

1.4 The relays

Voters never send anything to a server of ours. Every message travels as a signed event on public Nostr relays run by other people; this ledger names three. A relay is a dumb store-and-forward service: it sees the network address of whoever posts and the bytes they post, keeps a copy, and gives it to whoever asks. That copy is also what makes a dropped message provable (channels).

1.5 The ledger and its mirror

The ledger is our one server, the ingest. It reads the relays, checks every message, and appends what passes, and what fails with the reason, to one append-only log that it signs. It holds the log's signing key and nothing else of value: no decryption key, no voter secret. The log is published as plain files in a public git repository, the mirror, that browsers read by commit hash. The ingest is the only writer; anyone is a reader (the log, the mirror).

1.6 drand, the clock

drand is a public randomness beacon run by the League of Entropy: Cloudflare, EPFL, Kudelski Security, Protocol Labs, the Ethereum Foundation, UCL, the University of Chile and others. Every three seconds its nodes jointly produce a signature over the round number, using a threshold key that no single node holds. Two properties matter here. The signature of a future round does not exist until that round's time, and nobody can produce it alone. Once published, it is public for ever. drand's time-lock encryption turns the first property into a lock: encrypt to a round, and the round's signature is the key (tlock, paper). Nobody in the League knows their beacon is used for this poll.

1.7 Bitcoin, the calendar

The ledger proves when each state of its log existed by stamping it with OpenTimestamps, which commits a hash into a Bitcoin block. Bitcoin is only read for its block headers and written to only through the public stamping calendars; no ZEC and no BTC move (the log).

1.8 The authority and the auditor

The authority is whoever opens the ballots after the close and posts the count. Under a time-lock it holds no key: it fetches the drand round, opens every ballot with it, and posts the result, which the ingest re-checks before logging. Ours runs once an hour; anyone else can run one, and two correct ones produce the same entry. The auditor is anyone who recomputes everything from the log, the relays and the mirror, including your browser (the audit).

2. The vote, one step at a time

2.1 Setup: a ledger, a registry, a poll

Three small documents define a deployment, and all three are the first entries of the log, so a reader learns the rules from the same place as the results. The ledger entry names the log's origin, its signing key and the relays to use. A registry is an eligibility rule: here, any passport that ZKPassport can verify, unexpired, with no age or nationality condition, identified by the salted identifier under a pinned OPRF key. A poll names its registry, its question and options, its opening and closing times, the drand round that opens it, and the hash of the circuit that ballots must prove against. Once logged, a registry's identifier settings never change, since a different setting would give every passport a second identifier (ledger entry, registries, polls). The files of this deployment are in ledger/.

2.2 The voter secret

Before anything is proven, the browser needs one secret that will stand for the voter in every poll of the registry. Where the platform offers it, the secret is derived from a passkey through the WebAuthn PRF extension, so it lives in the device's secure hardware; otherwise it is twelve BIP39 words the voter writes down. The secret never leaves the device. Everything the voter later publishes is derived from it by one-way functions, so that nothing published can be traced back to it (voter secret).

2.3 Registration: one passport, one leaf

Registration adds one leaf, a commitment to the voter's secret, to the registry's Merkle tree. The step has to achieve two things at once: prove that a valid passport stands behind the leaf, and make sure that passport adds no second leaf.

  1. The browser draws a one-time key and shows a request to the phone that binds that key: the proof will be usable for this registration only, so a relay that sees it cannot reuse it for a leaf of its own.
  2. The phone reads the chip, checks the state's signature chain, performs the face match, obtains the salt from the OPRF network on a blinded query, and produces a zero-knowledge proof that discloses only: the document is valid and unexpired on this day, the request's one-time key, and the salted identifier. Name, number, photo, birth date and nationality stay on the phone (ZKPassport, personhood).
  3. The browser derives the leaf from the voter secret, signs the registration with the one-time key, and posts it to a relay.
  4. The ingest verifies the proof with ZKPassport's open verifier, checks that the proof's OPRF key is the registry's, refuses the identifier if the log already holds it, and otherwise appends the leaf at the next position of the tree. Every refusal is logged with its reason.

The identifier is public in the log. That is deliberate: it is what lets anyone, not only us, check that no passport registered twice. What it reveals is the subject of section 3.4 (registration).

2.4 The ballot and its proof

A ballot must show that it comes from a registered voter without saying which one, and that the same voter cannot cast a second one. Both come from one zero-knowledge proof, written in Noir and proven with Aztec's UltraHonk in the browser. The proof's public values are: a recent root of the registry tree; the poll; a pseudonym, derived one-way from the voter secret and the poll, so that it is the same for the voter's every attempt in this poll and unrelated to their pseudonym in any other poll; a commitment to the choice; and the hash of the sealed choice described next. The proof states that a leaf under that root was derived from the secret that also produced the pseudonym, and that the committed choice is exactly one of the poll's options or the blank. The ingest accepts one ballot per pseudonym per poll; a second one is refused and logged (the statement, the checks).

UltraHonk needs no ceremony of ours: it uses a universal reference string, and anyone can rebuild the circuit with the generator and compare the verification key hash the poll pins.

2.5 Sealing the choice until the close

The choice itself is not in the proof. It is sealed under a fresh symmetric key, and that key is encrypted with tlock to the drand round of the close. Until that round exists, nobody can open any ballot, including us and including the voter's own browser. The proof binds the hash of the sealed blob, so nobody can swap a voter's sealed choice for another without invalidating the proof. One subtlety is worth a sentence: the circuit commits to the choice but does not verify the encryption, because the time-lock's arithmetic is far too costly inside a proof. The check is deferred to the opening: a blob that does not open to its commitment makes the ballot void, listed and not counted, and only the voter's own software could have produced such a blob (encryption).

2.6 Transport and receipts

Registrations and ballots are posted to the relays as signed events under one-time keys. The voter's software ranks the relays by a value derived from the secret and sends the registration to the first and the ballots to the second, so that one relay does not see both. The ingest answers each message with a signed acknowledgement naming the checkpoint that will hold it, posted back to the relay the message came from. The browser keeps that receipt and later checks that the checkpoint does contain the message; a missing one is a complaint that anyone can judge from the relay's copy and the ingest's signature (acknowledgements, receipts).

2.7 The log, the mirror, the stamps

The log is an RFC 6962 Merkle tree, the structure behind Certificate Transparency. Every minute in which it grew, the ingest signs a checkpoint in the C2SP signed-note format: the log's size and root, the size and root of each registry tree, and the git commit of the mirror that holds the files. Entries inside a checkpoint are ordered by hash, not by arrival, so the public record dates nothing to the second. Each checkpoint is stamped with OpenTimestamps and the stamp is upgraded until it carries a Bitcoin attestation. The mirror is static files read by commit, so the vote page pins exactly what it read; the setups and the results carry inclusion proofs, so the mirror cannot serve a key or a result the log does not hold (the log, the mirror).

2.8 The close and the opening

At the first checkpoint after the closing time the ingest logs a close entry; no ballot is accepted after it. When the drand round arrives, the authority fetches the beacon, verifies its signature under the chain's public key, opens every counted ballot, checks each against its commitment, and posts the tally: the totals, the number counted, the list of void ballots and the beacon itself. The ingest repeats every opening before logging the entry. Because the beacon is public, anyone can repeat the whole computation, and two correct tallies are byte for byte the same (tally, close).

2.9 The audit

An audit replays the ledger's judgment from scratch: every checkpoint signature and the consistency between checkpoints, the stamps against Bitcoin headers, every registration proof and identifier, every ballot proof and pseudonym, every refusal's reason, the beacon and every opening, and that the mirror's files at each commit are the log's. With the relays, it also checks that every message they hold was judged, which is how withholding shows. A check that cannot run is a failure, not a pass. The same audit runs in the browser and from the command line with one script (the audit).

3. Privacy analysis

3.1 The three links

Everything a poll could leak about a voter is one of three links, and the protocol places each behind a different mechanism.

  • Passport to leaf. Does a given passport stand behind a given registration? The registration proof discloses only the salted identifier, so this link is as hidden as the identifier is unrecomputable.
  • Leaf to ballot. Which registration cast a given ballot? Hidden by the ballot's zero-knowledge membership proof, under the assumptions of UltraHonk on BN254.
  • Ballot to choice. What does a ballot say? Hidden until the drand round by the time-lock, and public afterwards, under the ballot's pseudonym.

The last point is the honest statement of what this design is: at the close, every ballot opens, pseudonymously. The pseudonym links to no one, but a voter who gives up their secret can prove which ballot is theirs. The protocol reveals choices without persons, not nothing.

3.3 What a corrupted party gains

The ledger. It holds no key that opens anything, so a corrupt ledger reads no more than the public. It cannot forge a registration, since every one needs a passport proof; cannot forge or alter a ballot, since every one needs a proof under a secret it does not hold; cannot drop a message quietly, since the relay keeps a copy and the voter keeps a receipt. What it can do is refuse service, and keep two signed histories to show different readers. That fork is detectable, not prevented: any two conflicting checkpoint notes prove it, and the audit collects notes from the relays and the mirror (limits, fork detection).

A relay. It pairs a network address with what was posted from it. For a registration, that is address and identifier. For a ballot, address and pseudonym, and at the round, the choice. Since registrations and ballots go to different relays, pairing an identifier with a choice through an address needs two relays, or one relay and a network observer. None of this reaches the person, unless the address itself does.

The OPRF servers. Below the threshold they learn nothing beyond the phone's address and the moment of a registration. At the threshold they reconstruct the key and can evaluate the function on any input; by itself that still yields nothing, because they do not know any passport's data. The danger is in combination, in the next section.

drand. A threshold of the League could produce a round's signature early. It would then read every ballot before the close, exactly as everyone does at the close: choices under pseudonyms, no persons. It cannot alter, forge or discard a ballot, and it cannot change the count, which anyone recomputes from the same beacon.

ZKPassport. Its circuits and verifier are open and pinned by hash; a flaw in them would be a flaw in eligibility, not in secrecy, and it would be public. Its bridge sees addresses, not content.

The voter's own devices. A seized or compromised device gives up the secret, and with it every pseudonym and ballot of the voter. The twelve words recover the secret; they do not revoke it. This is also the vote-buying surface: a voter who hands over the words lets a buyer check the ballot after the close.

3.4 What the issuing state can learn

The state is the natural adversary of a passport-gated poll: it knows every passport it issued, chip data included, and it can read the public log like anyone else. What it can learn depends on the identifier.

  • With a plain identifier, the state recomputes the identifier of each of its passports from its own records and the registry's labels, and reads off the list of its citizens who registered. It still cannot see how anyone voted. Zcash Agora does not use this mode.
  • With the salted identifier and an honest OPRF network, the state cannot recompute anything: the salt it would need is held in shares by servers that only ever answer blinded queries. The log tells it that some passports registered, not whose.
  • With the salted identifier and a corrupted OPRF threshold, state and servers together recompute every identifier: the servers supply the function, the state the inputs. The result is the same as the plain mode: the list of citizens who registered. Nothing more, because the OPRF touches registration only; the ballot's membership proof and the time-lock are unaffected, so the state still learns neither which ballot is a citizen's nor what it says.

So the strongest realistic adversary, a state colluding with a threshold of the OPRF network, learns participation and not choices. A state that also controls a relay and observes its citizens' networks could go one step further and pair an address with a choice, which is why the exposure that defeats this chain is the network one, and Tor or a VPN closes it.

3.5 What nobody can do

  • Register twice with one passport: the identifier is one per document for this registry, and the log refuses a second.
  • Vote twice in one poll: the pseudonym is one per secret and poll, and the log refuses a second.
  • Vote without registering: the ballot proof needs a leaf in the tree.
  • Alter a ballot in transit: the proof binds the sealed blob's hash.
  • Read a ballot before the round: the key does not exist.
  • Miscount: the tally is a deterministic function of the log and the beacon, and anyone recomputes it.
  • Drop a ballot unnoticed: the relay's copy and the voter's receipt prove the omission.
  • Rewrite history unnoticed: checkpoints are signed, consistent and timestamped in Bitcoin; a fork shows as two conflicting notes.

3.6 Limits

  • Ballots open in clear at the round, under their pseudonyms. This is what lets anyone recount without a key holder, and it is also why the time-lock mode allows one ballot per voter and no correction: a correction would open in clear too (the matrix).
  • A fork of the log is detectable, not prevented. Pinning one history on a chain is designed, not deployed (design).
  • Network metadata is the one exposure the protocol does not close by itself.
  • Passports only, with a chip and an NFC phone, and a face match for the salted identifier.
  • Not post-quantum. The time-lock is pairing-based and the proofs are on BN254. Since every ballot becomes public at the round anyway, a future quantum computer learns about choices nothing the public does not; it could, in principle, attack the proofs that hide the two other links.
  • Scale. About a hundred thousand voters and ballots with the current software; the tree holds a million leaves.
